## Persona in one sentence

The executive champion needs to move AI from scattered pilots into a controlled, reusable production capability that produces business value without creating agent sprawl, architectural fragility, or a governance failure.

## Role boundary

This persona mobilizes the technical and organizational system needed for production. It usually does not own the entire business benefit alone.

The executive champion is distinct from:

- The economic buyer, who owns or approves the operating and P&L outcome
- The functional champion, who owns the workflow, rules, baseline, and people
- The technical evaluator, who performs detailed architecture and implementation diligence
- Security, privacy, compliance, legal, and procurement approvers
- Operational users who review exceptions after launch

One person may cover more than one role, but writing should keep the decision logic separate.

## What this person owns

- The enterprise AI, automation, or digital-transformation portfolio
- The path from use-case discovery to production readiness
- Alignment between business value, architecture, governance, and delivery capacity
- Platform reuse and the prevention of incompatible one-off agent stacks
- Executive visibility into time to production, operating value, and risk posture
- A shared responsibility model across business, technology, risk, and vendor or partner teams
- The decision framework for when autonomy may expand and when humans remain in control

## Core pains

### 1. Pilot proliferation without production value

Business units can launch AI experiments faster than the enterprise can establish ownership, architecture, monitoring, and accountable operating processes.

### 2. The control gap widens as agents gain authority

A tool that recommends text is different from a system that classifies, validates, decides, writes to enterprise systems, and triggers downstream actions. Runtime control becomes a central design problem.

### 3. Internal builds consume scarce engineering capacity

An agent framework is not only a model call. Integration, evaluation, identity, permissions, observability, testing, governance, exception handling, and ongoing maintenance become a platform responsibility.

### 4. Existing automation reaches an exception ceiling

RPA, rules, OCR, and IDP may work on structured deterministic tasks but struggle with multilingual documents, contextual decisions, variable inputs, and end-to-end orchestration.

### 5. The business expects speed while risk functions expect proof

If the champion treats security and governance as late-stage approvals, the initiative can stall after technical success. If controls have no production path, the business sees technology leadership as the blocker.

## Current workarounds

These are **inferences**, not disclosed Otera customer inventories.

- Allow separate RPA, data-science, and business teams to run disconnected pilots
- Build an internal agent framework and discover that lifecycle and governance consume the roadmap
- Restrict AI to read-only copilots because action-taking controls remain unresolved
- Add manual review to every decision, preserving the labor model
- Continue using existing RPA or IDP despite high exception rates
- Depend on a systems integrator for delivery without building clear internal ownership

## Buying triggers

- The board or CEO demands measurable operating value from enterprise AI
- Business-led agent projects create visibility, security, or architecture concerns
- Existing automation cannot handle unstructured inputs and exceptions end to end
- A strong functional sponsor brings a high-volume workflow with measurable baseline data
- An internal build's time, maintenance, or specialist-talent cost becomes visible
- Regulation or internal audit requires clearer decision authority, traceability, and monitoring
- The organization needs a repeatable platform for multiple workflows, entities, or countries
- A trusted implementation partner offers delivery capacity and operating-model support

## Jobs to be done

### Functional jobs

- Give the business a governed route from agentic use case to production
- Establish a reusable execution and control layer across workflows
- Integrate autonomous operations with existing systems and orchestration
- Define ownership, authorization, monitoring, and escalation for action-taking agents
- Move from universal human review to evidence-led autonomy within explicit boundaries
- Turn one successful workflow into a scalable enterprise operating capability

### Emotional jobs — inference

- Avoid being accountable for an opaque, unsafe, or expensive AI failure
- Regain confidence that technology can accelerate the business without losing control
- Avoid the reputational cost of a transformation portfolio full of demos and no outcomes

### Social and organizational jobs — inference

- Be recognized as the enabler of responsible production AI, not only the gatekeeper
- Give the board and economic buyer a credible answer to governance and integration objections
- Align business, technology, and risk around one inspectable operating model

## Decision criteria

1. **Production architecture:** Can the platform perform and govern real workflow actions?
2. **Integration fit:** Can it connect to existing systems without forcing a core replacement?
3. **Identity and authority:** Are roles, permissions, decision boundaries, and overrides explicit?
4. **Observability and audit:** Can teams reconstruct what happened and why?
5. **Evaluation and change control:** Can workflows, rules, prompts, and models be tested and versioned safely?
6. **Operational ownership:** Who monitors, handles exceptions, changes logic, and supports production?
7. **Reuse:** Can components and controls repeat across workflows and geographies?
8. **Build-versus-buy economics:** What platform engineering, maintenance, and opportunity cost does Otera replace or add?
9. **Vendor and partner dependency:** Can internal capability grow without creating permanent services dependence?
10. **Business accountability:** Does the economic buyer own a measurable result rather than delegating value to IT?

## Objections and failure fears

| Objection or fear | What it is testing | Proof required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "We cannot govern agents that make consequential decisions." | Runtime authority and accountability | Thresholds, exception routing, audit logs, override, policy enforcement, and named owners |
| "This becomes a proprietary black box or another silo." | Architectural durability | API and integration design, identity, data flows, portability, ownership, and exit considerations |
| "Security and compliance claims are marketing." | Assurance quality | Independent certificates, scope, data-flow review, tenant isolation, retention, subprocessors, and contractual controls |
| "Business users will configure unsafe logic." | Segregation of duties and release discipline | RBAC, approval paths, test evidence, versioning, and controlled promotion to production |
| "The model will fail on real-world variance." | Reliability and operational safety | Representative evaluation set, error taxonomy, confidence thresholds, monitoring, rollback, and SLOs |
| "We can build this more cheaply." | Total ownership economics | Build-versus-buy model covering governance, integration, evaluation, operations, maintenance, and opportunity cost |
| "The first workflow cannot justify another enterprise platform." | Reuse and portfolio fit | Expansion blueprint, reusable components, platform boundaries, and decommissioning or coexistence plan |

## Proof hierarchy

1. Customer-specific architecture, security, governance, and responsibility design
2. Production evidence from a comparable regulated workflow
3. Representative-data evaluation with documented failure and exception behavior
4. Runtime control demonstration: permissions, rules, logs, review, override, and escalation
5. Build-versus-buy and operating-cost model
6. Reuse evidence across additional workflows or countries
7. Generic platform diagrams, benchmark scores, certifications without scope, or feature counts

## Stakeholder dynamics

### Natural allies

- Economic buyer with an urgent and measurable workflow
- Functional owner with domain experts and baseline data
- Enterprise architecture and platform engineering
- Data and AI teams responsible for evaluation and lifecycle practices
- CISO, privacy, compliance, legal, model risk, and internal audit when engaged early
- Automation CoE or qualified implementation partner with delivery capacity

### Likely tension

- Business leaders who interpret control work as delay when no clear production path is offered
- Central IT teams uncomfortable with business-configurable logic
- Security or risk teams brought in after the solution is already chosen
- Existing RPA, IDP, workflow, or data-platform owners defending current investments
- Economic buyers who expect IT to own benefits without supplying a process owner or baseline
- Transformation partners whose delivery boundaries are unclear

## Content strategy

### Lead with

- One concrete workflow and the runtime control model
- How business value, governance, architecture, and people fit into one operating system
- Human authority, decision traces, integration, monitoring, and lifecycle ownership
- A phased path from bounded human review to evidence-led higher autonomy
- Total build-and-operate burden, not license comparison alone

### Avoid

- Saying "no-code" without explaining permissions, approval, testing, and change control
- Treating security, privacy, architecture, or model-risk review as procurement friction
- Promising full autonomy as the starting condition
- Implying core-system replacement is required
- Presenting certifications as a substitute for customer-specific diligence
- Making IT solely accountable for a benefit the business has not baselined or owned

### High-value content formats

- Target architecture and integration brief
- Runtime governance and decision-rights map
- Production-readiness checklist
- Build-versus-buy and lifecycle TCO model
- Human-in-the-loop to STP ramp plan
- Shared responsibility and operating-model template
- Regulated-workflow reference architecture and customer proof
